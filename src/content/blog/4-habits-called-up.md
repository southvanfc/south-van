Every season, a handful of players move up: pulled into a higher age group, a competitive squad, or a first-team environment before anyone expected it. Talent gets you noticed. It's rarely the reason you actually get the call.

After years of coaching in Vancouver, the players who move up tend to share the same handful of habits, and most of them have nothing to do with being the most skilled kid on the field. Here's what we actually see.

---

## 1. They Do the Boring Stuff Without Being Told

You almost never have to tell these players to work harder. They're already at warm-up before you get the cones out. They finish reps the right way even when nobody's watching them do it. Fitness runs, shape work, the fortieth repetition of the same passing pattern... none of it gets half effort just because it's not a scrimmage.

Coaches notice this fast. If you're consistent when it's boring, a coach starts trusting you'll be consistent when it matters.

**Try this:** Pick one drill you usually go through the motions on. Give it full effort for two weeks straight and see what changes.

---

## 2. They Recover Fast From Mistakes

Every player makes mistakes: a bad touch, a missed tackle, a giveaway that leads to a goal. What separates players who move up isn't that they make fewer mistakes. It's how fast they reset after one.

Sulk for five minutes after a bad touch and you're a liability out there, plain and simple. But drop your shoulders, shake it off, and you're back in the play within a few seconds? That's the kind of thing coaches remember. Higher up, the game moves faster and mistakes happen more often, so a coach needs to know you won't check out every time one goes against you.

**Try this:** Next time you make a mistake in training, count to three, then sprint into your next positioning. Make the reset physical, not just mental.

---

## 3. They're Coachable, Not Just Talented

This is the biggest one. Raw ability gets a player noticed. Coachability is what gets them moved up and kept there.

Being coachable means actually changing something when a coach corrects you, instead of nodding and doing the same thing next rep. It means asking questions when you don't understand a tactical instruction instead of guessing. It means taking feedback in front of teammates without getting defensive.

Coaches remember which players actually apply what they're told. Those are the players they trust enough to hand more responsibility to.

**Try this:** After your next correction from a coach, apply it on the very next touch or rep, not eventually, immediately.

---

## 4. They Compete for Positions They Don't Usually Play

Coaches remember the kid who says yes when asked to play somewhere new. Not because he's good there right away, most players aren't, but because he doesn't hesitate. That single moment tells a coach more than a highlight reel ever could.

You can't fake that under pressure. Either you step into the unfamiliar or you find a reason not to, and coaches are watching for which one you do.

**Try this:** Ask your coach to throw you into an unfamiliar spot next scrimmage. Left back instead of striker, center mid instead of winger, whatever you never play. Get uncomfortable now, on purpose, so it's not a shock later.

---

## What This Actually Looks Like Over a Season

You won't see any of this in one practice. It shows up over months, in stuff nobody points out at the time. Did you still bust the last rep of a drill you're sick of running? What did your face do after that giveaway? Did you actually change something after the coach pulled you aside, or just nod and repeat the same mistake next week?

Talent gets you a look. Trust gets you the call. And trust isn't built off one good game, it's built off a coach watching the same things over and over and slowly deciding he can count on you when it matters.

If you're grinding it out somewhere in Vancouver, hoping to move up an age group or onto a better team, here's the part that should actually give you hope: every single one of these habits is something you control, starting tonight.

---

## Conclusion

None of these four habits require more talent than the player next to you. They require consistency, humility, and a willingness to be uncomfortable, all things you can start building in your very next training session.
